## What Is Plant and Machinery Valuation?

Plant and machinery valuation is the process of determining the fair and supportable value of industrial assets such as production machines, factory equipment, boilers, generators, electrical installations, tools, and utilities. The work is carried out by a qualified **valuer** who inspects the assets, studies their condition and records, and then estimates their value as on a specific date and for a specific purpose.

In simple terms, **valuation of plant and machinery** answers one question: *what is this asset worth today?* The answer depends on the purpose. The value of a machine for a bank loan can differ from its value for insurance, a sale, or a scrap auction. That is why every valuation report must clearly state the **basis of value** and the **premise of value** it follows.

The terms **plant valuation** and **machinery valuation** are often used together. Strictly speaking, "plant" refers to the complete installation or facility used in a business (for example, a textile mill or a cement unit), while "machinery" refers to the individual machines and equipment within it. A professional plant and machinery valuation normally covers both, including civil foundations for machinery, utilities, and erection and commissioning costs where relevant.

### Common Assets Covered

- CNC machines, lathes, presses, and machine tools
- Textile, hosiery, and spinning machinery
- Food processing and packaging lines
- Boilers, turbines, DG sets, transformers, and compressors
- Cranes, material handling equipment, and forklifts
- Laboratory and testing equipment
- Electrical panels, piping, and process utilities
- Complete factories and plants (going concern or closed units)

## Need and Purpose of Plant and Machinery Valuation

Businesses and individuals need valuation of plant and machinery for many practical, financial, and legal reasons.

1. **Bank loans and finance.** Banks and NBFCs require a [valuation report for loans and security](/#services) to fix the loan amount, to take machinery as primary or collateral security, and for periodic re-valuation of hypothecated assets.
2. **Insurance.** A valuation helps decide the correct sum insured. Under-insurance can reduce claim payouts, while over-insurance means paying unnecessary premium.
3. **Buying or selling machinery.** Both buyers and sellers use **old machinery valuation** (see [valuation for sale and purchase](/machinery-valuation-sale-purchase/)) to arrive at a fair price for second-hand equipment.
4. **Mergers, acquisitions, and demergers.** Asset values are needed to determine the value of the business and to fix share exchange ratios.
5. **Insolvency and liquidation.** Under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, valuation (see our page on [valuation for IBC](/ibc-valuation/)) is needed to determine the fair value and liquidation value of a company's assets.
6. **Financial reporting.** Companies use valuation for asset revaluation, impairment testing, purchase price allocation, and compliance with accounting standards (Ind AS or AS).
7. **Taxation.** Valuation supports depreciation claims, capital gains computation, and transfer of assets between related parties.
8. **Issue of shares for non-cash consideration.** When machinery is brought in as capital against shares, a registered valuer's report is generally required.
9. **Partnership changes, family settlements, and dispute resolution.** Valuation provides an independent figure when partners exit or assets are divided.
10. **Auction, disposal, and scrap sale.** Companies need realisable value or scrap value before disposing of obsolete assets.
11. **Customs and import of second-hand machinery.** A valuation or chartered engineer's certificate is often needed to support the declared value and remaining life.
12. **Government schemes and subsidies.** Many capital subsidy and incentive schemes ask for an independent valuation of the machinery purchased.

## Methods of Plant and Machinery Valuation

International and Indian valuation standards recognise three main approaches. A good **registered valuer** selects the approach that best suits the asset and the purpose, and often uses more than one to cross-check the result.

### 1. Cost Approach

This is the most widely used method for **plant valuation** and machinery valuation, especially where comparable sales are scarce. It is based on the principle that a buyer will not pay more for an asset than the cost of acquiring an equivalent one.

**Steps involved:**

- Estimate the **Replacement Cost New (RCN)** or **Reproduction Cost New**, meaning the cost of a new, similar machine today, including freight, taxes, installation, and commissioning.
- Deduct **depreciation** for physical deterioration, functional obsolescence, and economic (external) obsolescence.
- The result is the **Depreciated Replacement Cost (DRC)**.

In formula form: *Value = Replacement Cost New – (Physical Depreciation + Functional Obsolescence + Economic Obsolescence)*

Cost indexation and the capacity-based scaling method (often called the six-tenths rule) are sometimes used to estimate RCN when the exact model is no longer manufactured.

### 2. Market Approach

The market approach estimates value by comparing the machine with similar machines recently sold or offered in the market. It works well for common, standardised equipment such as generators, lathes, forklifts, and commercial vehicles.

- Collect data on comparable sales, dealer quotes, auction results, and listings.
- Adjust for differences in age, make, model, capacity, condition, location, and accessories.
- Arrive at an indicated value from the adjusted comparables.

This method is very useful for **old machinery valuation**, where an active second-hand market exists.

### 3. Income Approach

The income approach values an asset based on the income or cash flow it is expected to generate over its remaining life. Techniques include the Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) method and income capitalisation.

It is used when machinery is a major income-producing asset of a business, such as a power plant, a wind turbine, or a dedicated production line. For most individual machines it is less practical because income cannot easily be separated from the rest of the business.

### Bases (Standards) of Value

The same machine can have different values depending on the basis chosen. The common bases are:

- **Fair Market Value:** price between a willing buyer and a willing seller, both well informed and acting without compulsion.
- **Fair Value:** value as defined by accounting or legal standards for reporting or transactions.
- **Value in Use / Going Concern Value:** value of the asset as part of a running business.
- **Orderly Liquidation Value:** value when the asset is sold in an organised manner over a reasonable period.
- **Forced Sale (Distress) Value:** value when the asset must be sold quickly, usually at a discount.
- **Salvage or Scrap Value:** value of the asset for its material content after its useful life.
- **Insurable Value:** value used to fix the sum insured, often based on replacement cost.

## Factors Affecting the Value of Plant and Machinery

Several technical, commercial, and market factors influence the final value arrived at by a valuer.

1. **Age and remaining useful life.** Older machines generally have lower values. The remaining economic life is more important than chronological age alone.
2. **Physical condition.** Wear and tear, corrosion, damage, leakages, vibration, and the standard of past repair work all matter.
3. **Maintenance history.** Regular servicing, availability of logbooks, and replacement of critical parts increase value. Poorly maintained machines are discounted.
4. **Make, model, and brand.** Reputed manufacturers and models with good service support hold value better.
5. **Capacity and utilisation.** Installed capacity, actual output, shift pattern, and hours of operation affect the degree of wear and the machine's earning ability.
6. **Technology and obsolescence.** Machines using outdated technology lose value because newer models are faster, more efficient, or more automated. This is called functional obsolescence.
7. **Availability of spare parts and service.** If spares are unavailable or the manufacturer has discontinued support, value falls.
8. **Demand in the second-hand market.** A strong resale market for a particular type of machine supports higher value.
9. **Economic and industry conditions.** A slowdown in the industry, import duties, or changes in demand can reduce the value (economic obsolescence).
10. **Installation and location.** Machines that are installed, commissioned, and working are valued differently from those lying dismantled. Transport and dismantling costs matter for in-situ versus ex-situ value.
11. **Statutory and environmental compliance.** Pollution control norms, safety rules, and energy-efficiency standards can affect whether a machine can continue to be used.
12. **Original cost, import content, and exchange rate.** For imported machinery, currency movement, duties, and freight affect replacement cost.
13. **Warranty, certifications, and legal status.** Clear title, absence of charges or liens, and valid certificates (such as boiler certification) support value.
14. **Purpose and premise of valuation.** Continued use, removal for sale, forced sale, or scrap each gives a different value.

## Documents Required for Plant and Machinery Valuation

Providing complete documents helps the valuer complete the work faster and more accurately. The exact list varies by purpose, but the following are commonly required. For a client-focused checklist, see [documents required from you for valuation](/articles/documents-required-plant-machinery-valuation/).

- Purchase invoices and proforma invoices of machinery (original cost and date of purchase)
- Fixed asset register with asset-wise details and book values
- Import documents, bill of entry, and customs papers for imported machinery
- Technical specifications, machine catalogues, and capacity details
- Details of make, model, serial number, year of manufacture, and rated capacity
- Installation, commissioning, and foundation details
- Maintenance records, service history, and major repair or overhaul details
- Machine list with asset tag numbers and location
- Layout plan of the factory or plant
- Existing insurance policy copies
- Latest audited balance sheet and depreciation schedule
- Production and capacity utilisation data
- Loan sanction letter and details of any charge or hypothecation (for bank valuations)
- Statutory certificates such as pollution control consent, factory licence, and boiler or electrical safety certificates
- Photographs, if available, and the contact details of the plant in-charge for site inspection
- Identity and entity documents of the owner or company (PAN, GST registration, incorporation documents)

A site visit is essential. The valuer physically verifies each asset, checks its condition and working status, and compares it with the records provided.

## Process of Plant and Machinery Valuation

1. **Engagement and scope.** The client states the purpose, valuation date, and assets to be covered.
2. **Collection of documents** and preliminary data.
3. **Physical inspection** of the plant and machinery, including identification, condition assessment, and photographs.
4. **Market research** to collect new machine prices, dealer quotes, and comparable data.
5. **Analysis and calculation** using the chosen valuation approach.
6. **Report preparation.** The valuation report states the basis of value, assumptions, limiting conditions, methodology, asset-wise values, and the final conclusion.
7. **Signing and submission** by the registered valuer or authorised valuer, following applicable standards.

## Who Is a Registered Valuer?

A **registered valuer** is a person who is enrolled with a Registered Valuers Organisation (RVO) and registered with the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI) in a specified asset class, after meeting the prescribed qualifications, experience, and examination requirements. In India, the recognised asset classes are:

- **Plant and Machinery**
- Land and Building
- Securities or Financial Assets

A person qualified in the plant and machinery asset class can issue valuation reports for purposes where the law requires a registered valuer. Many people also search for terms like **authorised valuer**, **approved valuer**, or **government approved valuer**. In practice, these usually refer to a valuer who is empanelled with a particular bank, insurance company, or department, or who holds registration under the law. When you need a report for a statutory purpose, always confirm that the valuer holds a valid registration with IBBI in the relevant asset class.

Valuers also commonly hold qualifications in engineering (mechanical, electrical, production), and may be **chartered engineers** or members of professional bodies such as the Institution of Engineers (India).

## Regulatory Framework: Companies Act and Regulating Bodies

### Companies Act, 2013

**Section 247** of the Companies Act, 2013 provides that where a valuation of any property, stocks, shares, debentures, securities, goodwill, or any other assets (or net worth of a company or its liabilities) is required under the Act, it must be made by a person who is a **registered valuer**. The valuer must be appointed by the audit committee or, in its absence, by the Board of Directors of the company.

Section 247 also sets out the duties of the valuer. The valuer must make an impartial, true, and fair valuation, exercise due diligence, follow the prescribed valuation standards, and must not undertake valuation of assets in which he or she has a direct or indirect interest, or becomes interested, during the three years before appointment or the three years after the valuation was conducted. Penalties apply for contravention, and a false or misleading valuation can attract punishment.

Some other provisions of the Act where a registered valuer's report may be needed include:

- **Section 62(1)(c):** preferential allotment of shares, including shares issued for consideration other than cash
- **Section 192:** restrictions on non-cash transactions involving directors
- **Sections 230 to 232:** compromises, arrangements, mergers, and amalgamations
- **Section 281:** report by the company liquidator on valuation of assets in winding up (and related provisions in liquidation proceedings)
- Other sections dealing with the purchase of own securities, reduction of capital, and related matters

### Companies (Registered Valuers and Valuation) Rules, 2017

These rules, issued by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs, lay down the eligibility, qualifications, experience, and examination requirements for registered valuers. They also cover the following:

- Registration with IBBI through a Registered Valuers Organisation
- Asset classes in which a valuer can be registered
- The code of conduct for valuers
- The requirement to follow valuation standards
- Penalties and disciplinary action for misconduct

### Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI)

IBBI is the authority that regulates registered valuers under the Companies Act, 2013 and the 2017 Rules. It grants registration, supervises Registered Valuers Organisations, and can take disciplinary action. Under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, valuations of a corporate debtor's assets (fair value and liquidation value) are carried out by registered valuers appointed by the resolution professional or liquidator.

### Registered Valuers Organisations (RVOs)

RVOs are professional bodies recognised by IBBI that enrol valuers, conduct training and examinations, and monitor their conduct. Examples include organisations promoted by or associated with institutions such as ICAI, ICSI, ICMAI, the Institution of Valuers, and other professional associations, each recognised for one or more asset classes.

### Valuation Standards

Valuers are expected to follow recognised valuation standards, including the **ICAI Valuation Standards** and the **International Valuation Standards (IVS)** issued by the International Valuation Standards Council. These standards guide the bases of value, valuation approaches, documentation, and reporting. IBBI also issues its own standards and report formats for valuations under the IBC, so check the IBBI website for the current ones.

### Other Relevant Laws and Authorities

- **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016** for liquidation and resolution valuation
- **SARFAESI Act, 2002** and bank guidelines for valuation of secured assets
- **Income Tax Act, 1961** for depreciation, capital gains, and related provisions
- **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** guidelines for bank and NBFC lending and valuer empanelment
- **Insurance regulations (IRDAI)** and insurer requirements for insured value
- **SEBI regulations** where valuation is required for listed companies
- **Customs and DGFT rules** for import of second-hand machinery
- **Accounting standards (Ind AS and AS)** for revaluation, impairment, and fair value measurement

*Note: Rules, section numbers, and thresholds are amended from time to time. Always confirm the current position with IBBI, the Ministry of Corporate Affairs, or a professional before relying on it for a specific transaction.*

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the difference between plant valuation and machinery valuation?**
Plant valuation covers the whole installation, including utilities and supporting systems, while machinery valuation focuses on individual machines. Most reports cover both.

**How is old machinery valued?**
Old machinery valuation usually combines the cost approach (replacement cost less depreciation and obsolescence) with the market approach (prices of similar second-hand machines), after an inspection of condition and remaining life.

**Is a registered valuer compulsory for plant and machinery valuation?**
Where the Companies Act or another law requires a registered valuer, yes. For private purposes such as buying a used machine, a registered valuer is not mandatory, but a qualified valuer's report adds credibility and is often accepted by banks and insurers.

**What documents are required for valuation?**
Purchase invoices, the fixed asset register, machine specifications, maintenance records, insurance papers, and statutory certificates are typical. See the full list above.

**How long does a valuation take?**
A small unit may take a few days, while a large plant can take longer, depending on the number of assets and the availability of documents.

**Does valuation differ from book value?**
Yes. Book value is the figure after accounting depreciation, while valuation reflects current market and technical reality. The two can be quite different.
